# Why Galois connections are not about fields

The phrase "Galois connection" sounds like it should live inside field
theory and nowhere else. It does not. The field-theoretic
correspondence — subgroups of a Galois group against intermediate fields
— is one instance of a two-line definition about partially ordered sets,
and the same definition also owns the floor function, the subgroup
generated by a set, the topological closure, and, one level up, the
adjoint functor theorems.

The reason to care is economy of proof. A Galois connection is a pair of
maps between two posets that answer each other's questions
([[def-galois-connection]]). From that single equivalence, and nothing
else, one gets:

- round trips that only ever move you one way
  ([[prp-galois-round-trips]]);
- a preservation theorem — one adjoint keeps suprema, the other keeps
  infima ([[thm-adjoints-preserve-bounds]]);
- a closure operator for free, whose closed elements are exactly the
  image of one of the maps ([[thm-connection-induces-closure]]).

Each of those, once proved, applies everywhere the pattern is found.
That $\lfloor \min(x,y) \rfloor = \min(\lfloor x \rfloor, \lfloor y
\rfloor)$, that $\langle S \rangle$ is idempotent, and that
abelianization turns a free product into a direct sum are not three
facts to memorize; they are one theorem wearing three costumes
([[exm-floor-ceiling-adjunction]],
[[exm-generated-subgroup-closure]], [[exm-abelianization-left-adjoint]]).

The ladder is short: posets first, Galois's own example in the middle,
categories at the top ([[con-galois-connections-are-adjunctions]]).
Nothing on the first two rungs needs any category theory, and the third
rung only renames what the first two already proved.
