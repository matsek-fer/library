# Galois connections beyond Galois theory

The phrase "Galois connection" sounds like it should live inside field
theory and nowhere else. In fact the field-theoretic correspondence is one
instance of a two-line order-theoretic definition that also owns the floor
function, generated subgroups, topological closures, and — one level up —
the adjoint functor theorems. This post walks the ladder: posets first,
Galois's own example in the middle, categories at the top.

## Two maps facing each other

Let $P$ and $Q$ be partially ordered sets. A **Galois connection** between
them is a pair of monotone maps $f \colon P \to Q$ and $g \colon Q \to P$
such that for all $p \in P$, $q \in Q$:

$$
f(p) \le q \iff p \le g(q).
$$

We call $f$ the **lower (left) adjoint** and $g$ the **upper (right)
adjoint**. The equivalence says the two maps answer each other's questions:
any question about $f(p)$ from above is a question about $p$ under $g$, and
each of $f$, $g$ determines the other completely.

Two consequences fall straight out of the definition. First,
$p \le g(f(p))$ (take $q = f(p)$) and $f(g(q)) \le q$ (take $p = g(q)$):
round trips only push you up on the $P$ side and down on the $Q$ side.
Second, and this is the workhorse: **the left adjoint preserves whatever
suprema exist, the right adjoint preserves whatever infima exist.** Proof
for $f$ and a supremum $s = \sup S$: for any $q$,
$f(s) \le q \iff s \le g(q) \iff (\forall p \in S)\, p \le g(q) \iff
(\forall p \in S)\, f(p) \le q$, so $f(s)$ has exactly the defining
property of $\sup f(S)$. Nothing about either poset was used — only the
equivalence.

## Floor and ceiling

The smallest interesting example is the inclusion
$\iota \colon \mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$. Does it have adjoints?
Asking for a right adjoint $g$ means asking: for real $x$, which integer
$g(x)$ satisfies $\iota(n) \le x \iff n \le g(x)$ for every integer $n$? The
integers below $x$ are exactly the integers below its integer part — so
$g = \lfloor \cdot \rfloor$, and

$$
n \le x \iff n \le \lfloor x \rfloor
$$

*is* the floor function, packaged as one equivalence instead of a
case-by-case definition. Symmetrically the ceiling is the *left* adjoint of
the same inclusion: $\lceil x \rceil \le n \iff x \le n$.

The preservation theorem now pays out for free. Floor, a right adjoint,
preserves infima: $\lfloor \min(x, y) \rfloor = \min(\lfloor x \rfloor,
\lfloor y \rfloor)$, and likewise for any family with an infimum. It has no
duty toward suprema, and indeed fails them: the set
$\{1 - \tfrac{1}{n} : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ has supremum $1$, but every
element has floor $0$, so the supremum of the floors is $0 \ne 1 =
\lfloor 1 \rfloor$. The asymmetry is not a defect of floor; it is the
signature of a one-sided adjoint.

## Subgroups and fixed fields

Now the eponymous example. Let $L/K$ be a finite Galois extension with
group $G = \operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$. On one side, the subgroups $H \le G$
ordered by inclusion; on the other, the intermediate fields
$K \subseteq M \subseteq L$, also by inclusion. The two maps are

$$
H \mapsto L^{H} \quad (\text{the fixed field}), \qquad
M \mapsto \operatorname{Gal}(L/M) \quad (\text{the fixing group}),
$$

and they satisfy, for every $H$ and $M$:

$$
M \subseteq L^{H} \iff H \subseteq \operatorname{Gal}(L/M),
$$

since both sides say precisely "every element of $H$ fixes every element of
$M$". One wrinkle: both maps are order-*reversing* — a bigger subgroup
fixes a smaller field. This is an **antitone** Galois connection, which is
the same thing as a monotone one after flipping one of the two posets
upside down, so the whole theory applies with sups and infs swapped on that
side. The fundamental theorem of Galois theory is then the statement that
for a finite Galois extension this connection is a *bijection* between all
subgroups and all intermediate fields — the special, rigid case. The
connection itself exists with no hypotheses at all; every antitone pair
built from a relation "$\sigma$ fixes $m$" works this way, which is
Galois theory's exportable skeleton.

## Every connection hides a closure operator

Compose the two maps of a connection: $c = g \circ f \colon P \to P$. The
round-trip facts above make $c$ **extensive** ($p \le c(p)$) and, with
monotonicity and the equivalence, **idempotent** ($c(c(p)) = c(p)$). A map
with these three properties is a **closure operator**, and its fixed points
— the "closed" elements — are exactly the image of $g$.

Run this on a connection between subsets and subgroups of a group $G$: the
left adjoint sends a subset $S$ to the generated subgroup
$\langle S \rangle$ (smallest subgroup above it — the defining equivalence
$\langle S \rangle \subseteq H \iff S \subseteq H$ is its universal
property), the right adjoint sends a subgroup to its underlying set. The
induced closure operator is $S \mapsto \langle S \rangle$, and the closed
sets are precisely the subgroups. The same template produces the span of a
set of vectors, the ideal generated by a ring subset, the topological
closure, and the deductive closure of a set of axioms in logic — one
theorem, many syllabi. In the Galois-theoretic example the two composites
$H \mapsto \operatorname{Gal}(L/L^H)$ and $M \mapsto L^{\operatorname{Gal}(L/M)}$
are both closure operators, and the fundamental theorem says exactly that
everything is already closed.

## The categorical upgrade

A poset is a category with at most one morphism between any two objects; a
monotone map is a functor; and the defining equivalence
$f(p) \le q \iff p \le g(q)$ is then a bijection of hom-sets
$\operatorname{Hom}(f(p), q) \cong \operatorname{Hom}(p, g(q))$ — the
definition of an **adjunction**. Galois connections *are* adjoint functor
pairs between thin categories. Under this dictionary, infima and suprema
become limits and colimits, and the poset preservation theorem becomes the
general one: **right adjoints preserve limits, left adjoints preserve
colimits**, by the same one-line argument of moving the question across the
adjunction.

Here is the theorem earning its keep outside posets. Abelianization
$G \mapsto G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/[G,G]$ is left adjoint to the inclusion
$\mathbf{Ab} \hookrightarrow \mathbf{Grp}$: homomorphisms
$G^{\mathrm{ab}} \to A$ into an abelian group correspond exactly to
homomorphisms $G \to A$. Being a left adjoint, it must preserve colimits —
in particular coproducts, which in $\mathbf{Grp}$ are free products and in
$\mathbf{Ab}$ are direct sums. So without any element-level computation,
$(G * H)^{\mathrm{ab}} \cong G^{\mathrm{ab}} \oplus H^{\mathrm{ab}}$: for
instance $(\mathbb{Z}/2 * \mathbb{Z}/3)^{\mathrm{ab}} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2
\oplus \mathbb{Z}/3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/6$, the abelianization of
$\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ read off in one line. And it explains a
refusal, too: abelianization is under no obligation to preserve limits, and
indeed fails them — it happens to preserve finite products, but not
infinite ones, and not kernels: the sign map $S_3 \to \mathbb{Z}/2$ has
kernel $A_3$, whose abelianization is $\mathbb{Z}/3$, while the abelianized
map is an isomorphism with trivial kernel — exactly as floor shrugged at
suprema. Floor preserving minima, generated-subgroup preserving unions of
chains, and abelianization preserving free products are one theorem wearing
three costumes; that is what the connection bought us.
